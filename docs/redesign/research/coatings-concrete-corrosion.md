# Coatings, concrete and corrosion: the physics behind the round-I notes

Date: 2026-11-07. Scope: the standards and building physics behind the user's round-I detail notes on the danchi (a
1960s–80s UR/公団-style RC block: concrete walls under spray tile or lithin, later elastic repaints; steel rails and
doors in enamel). The notes asked for faint darker, cooler flecks and embedded pebbles, more saturation variety,
pits, paint cracking and peeling down to concrete, vertical rust on the uprights, dark old and bright new rust,
exposed metal, and more attention at Derelict's top edge, top corners, corners and recesses. It adds what the rust
note (`weathering-rust-water.md`), the cracks note (`weathering-cracks-edges-damage.md`), `rE-concrete.md`, the
organic note (`weathering-organic-growth.md`) and `r3-believable-textures.md` lack. Rules: `weather_rules.json` 1.6.

## 0. Summary

1. **Pits are normal.** Coated-plywood forms (konpane) at w/c 0.55 left 1.25–1.82 % of the face as voids ≥ 1 mm,
   up to 5–6.5 mm deep [M 8]; ACI gives up to 25 mm, usually ≤ 15 mm, on vertical faces [C 7], densest high in
   each lift [M 8, 9]. About 5–40 per m² are 1 cm or more [I]; fillers and spray tile hide those under ~3 mm.
2. **The darker flecks have a name: aggregate transparency**, "darkened areas over coarse aggregate particles
   immediately below the concrete surface" [C 6]. Glossy, dense forms and porous aggregate favour it [C 7]. The
   aggregate was rounded mixed river gravel before about 1970, crushed hard sandstone or andesite after [P 11;
   snippet 12]. Pebbles show only where the paste skin has gone [C 7, 32].
3. **Coatings fail in a fixed order** [C 15]: stage 1 the film's surface (soiling, fading, gloss loss, chalking,
   wear), stage 2 the film (blisters, cracks, flaking), stage 3 the substrate (cracks, spalls, efflorescence, rust
   stains). On spray tile the top coat chalks, cracks and flakes first, showing the lighter main coat; then the main
   coat cracks, lifts and flakes to concrete.
4. **ISO 4628 gives the ladder.** Flaking classes 1–5 cover 0.1/0.3/1/3/15 % of the area with flakes up to
   1/3/10/30 mm and beyond; type a is coat from coat, type b the system from the substrate [C 2]. Cracks are just
   visible at ≤ 0.2 mm (size 2) and over 1 mm at size 5; depth a stays in the top coat, c goes through [C 1]. One
   Japanese city's survey calls a full repaint at degree 2–3 of blisters, cracks or peeling on ≥ 20 % of a face [P 17].
5. **Peeling starts where water stays or rain does not rinse**: balcony and stair soffits, parapet and upstand tops,
   around cracks and joints, and on recoats over chalky or dirty layers [P 16, 19; C 15, 18]. Concrete shows only
   in type b failures.
6. **Rust does not come through intact enamel** (the user's reading is right). It starts at breaks and thin film
   (arrises, welds and spatter, profile peaks, chips, crevices, mill scale left under paint) [C 27, 29, 30], then
   creeps under the film at 0.1–3.5 mm a year (rust note §2). Each patch sits on a defect, ringed by lifted or lost
   paint showing primer, older colours and a little bare metal [I].
7. **One rail spans L* 25–77.** Bare steel is a metal (F0 linear 0.53/0.51/0.49, L* ≈ 77) and mill scale is
   bluish-black [C 24, 30]; rust runs from fresh orange (L* 50–56) through mid (36–44) and old (28–34) to black inner
   rust (≈ 25) (rust note §6). The oldest, darkest rust sits where steel stays wettest (feet, joints, bottom rail),
   the brightest at growing fronts [I].
8. **Vertical runs are real.** Uprights drain the top rail; rust in that film stains the upright below each rusted
   joint (0.3–1.5 m in Japanese photos; 5–40 cm on the Soshigaya rails; whole uprights on derelict stairs: rust
   note §3). Horizontal rails corrode; uprights mostly stain until late.
9. **Japan is mostly C2–C3**: carbon steel lost a mean of about 180 g/m² (23 µm) in its first year across 25 sites
   [M 20]; moderate-salinity coasts are C4 (snippet) [23]. At the coast sheltered steel corrodes more than
   rain-washed steel [M 21].
10. **Top corners are the wettest part of a facade**, with up to 20–30× the rain of the face centre [C 32]. On
    limestone that washes them white [M 34]; on neglected paint in humid Japan I expect biofilm instead [I, with
    organic note M3 and snippet 36]. Tops that hold water whiten and blister the coat [P 19]; corner concrete
    carbonates 1.2–2× faster (snippet) [35]. Run-off gathers in inside corners [C 32]; set-backs escape rain
    washing (snippet) [37].

## 1. Formed concrete: pits, aggregate and mottling

**Bugholes (beyond `rE-concrete.md` Table 2).**
- **ACI 309.2R** [C 7]: regular or irregular cavities from barely visible up to 25 mm, usually ≤ 15 mm; most
  likely on vertical faces, with bleed-water voids at column tops and on battered faces. Sticky or stiff mixes,
  excess fines, too much vibration amplitude and part-inserted vibrators raise them.
- **Japanese test panels** [M 8]: coated plywood, w/c 0.55, slump 12 cm, air 4.5 %, voids ≥ 1 mm. Area 1.25–1.82 %
  (mean 1.53 %); maximum depth 5.0–6.5 mm (mean 5.9). With a better form face, most of the few voids left lay near
  the casting face. The panels were rodded, not vibrated, so this is the high end of ordinary walls (0.4–3 %, rE A3).
- **Upper layers** get less compaction from the concrete's own weight, so their skin is less dense: a void-removal
  device cut carbonation there by over 20 %, more than in the lower layer [M 9].
- **Measurement** [M 8–10]: photos or sheet transfers, binarised; voids ≥ 1 mm circle-equivalent, counted in classes
  1–5 mm and ≥ 5 mm; one inclined-wall trial counted from the form's mid-height up (snippet) [10].
- **Counts** [I]: area 1.5 % with N(>d) ∝ d^−b, b = 2–2.5, over 1–15 mm, gives about 3,500–5,000 voids ≥ 1 mm,
  90–140 ≥ 5 mm and 16–35 ≥ 10 mm per m². At 0.5 % area, a third of that.

**Aggregate.**
- **A 2019 Tokyo-area mix** [M 8]: 979 kg/m³ of crushed hard sandstone from Ome, max 20 mm (about 37 % of the
  volume), with Oi River sand.
- **History.** Gravel was 80.5 % of Japan's aggregate supply in FY1965 and 54.2 % in FY1973 (snippet) [12]. River
  gravel dominated into the 1960s; crushed stone, mostly sandstone and andesite, grew from the 1970s, earlier in
  western Japan, which also used sea sand [P 11]. So 1960s blocks hold rounded mixed gravel (grey sandstone, black
  slate and chert, white quartz, greenish rock); 1970s–80s blocks angular dark-grey stone [I].
- **Aggregate transparency**: dark or light patches the size and shape of the coarse aggregate; causes are low
  fines, dry or porous aggregate, high slump, and dense or glossy form faces [C 7], which describes coated konpane
  [I]. Spot cover [I]: at 37 % aggregate and 10 mm particles there are about 7 × 10⁵
  particles per m³; those whose near face lies 1–3 mm behind the skin give 1,000–2,000 spots per m², 2–8 % cover.
- **When pebbles show**: only where the paste skin has gone: rain-eroded tops, worn steps, spall floors (cracks
  note S9: 5–25 mm), honeycomb. Sand streaking exposes fine aggregate where bleed water ran up the form [C 7].
  Dirt collects in the matrix more than on the stones, less on rounded ones [C 32]. A thick finish with no top coat
  (stucco) loses its own aggregate with age [C 15].

**Mottling.**
- **ACI** [C 7]: colour variation shows within hours of stripping; it comes from incomplete mixing, vibrators too
  close to the form, and uneven absorption or release agent. Layer lines are dark horizontal lines between
  placements.
- **Others**: a lower surface w/c is darker; calcium chloride, hard troweling and uneven curing mottle (snippet)
  [13]. Dark mottling is worst with non-absorbent facings and in winter casting (snippet) [14].
- **Sizes by cause** (none measured) [I]: aggregate spots 5–25 mm, mixing and paste blotches 2–10 cm, pour and lift
  clouds 0.3–1.8 m (rE §1.5). Dark spots are denser paste, so cooler and blue-grey (rE A26). Concrete chroma stays
  at C* 1–4 (rE §1.5); saturation variety comes from aggregate colours, iron-stained sand and rust specks [I].

**What a coat does to pits.**
- **Practice** [P 19]: laitance and efflorescence are removed; offsets, honeycomb and cold joints are levelled with
  resin-cement mortar, and bugholes in porous substrates take a cement filler. US practice also fills bugholes
  (SSPC-SP13, snippet) [31]. Unfilled holes outgas into pinholes and holidays in the film [C 31]; smooth finishes
  show pinholes and unevenness from bugholes unless puttied [P 19].
- **Thickness decides** [I, from rE thicknesses]: paint (25–76 µm a coat) lines a pit, and its rim can catch light;
  elastomerics (0.3–0.5 mm) bridge about 1 mm; filler plus spray tile (about 1 mm mean, peaks 2–4 mm) hides pits
  under ~3 mm, and larger ones print as soft dimples at 30–60 % of their depth.

## 2. Coating degradation on concrete

**Grading standards.** ISO 4628 parts write results as quantity(S size) and depth, e.g. "2(S3)b".
- **Cracking, ISO 4628-4:2016** [C 1]. Quantity 0–5: none, very few, few, moderate, considerable, dense pattern.
  Size: 0 not visible at ×10; 1 visible only under ×10; 2 just visible (≤ 0.2 mm); 3 clearly visible (0.2–0.5 mm);
  4 large (0.5–1 mm); 5 very large (> 1 mm). Depth: a, cracks within the top coat ("checking"); b, through the top
  coat; c, through the whole system. Annex patterns include irregular and long-line cracking.
- **Flaking, ISO 4628-5:2022** [C 2]. This confirms the classes the cracks note (S14) quoted from memory.

  | Class | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | flaked area | 0 % | 0.1 % | 0.3 % | 1 % | 3 % | 15 % |
  | size, largest dimension | not visible at ×10 | ≤ 1 mm | ≤ 3 mm | ≤ 10 mm | ≤ 30 mm | > 30 mm |

  Type a: coat(s) flaking from an underlying coat. Type b: the whole system flaking from the substrate.
- **Others (snippets)**: ISO 4628-2 shows blisters at sizes 2–5 and densities 2–5 [3]; ISO 4628-6 rates chalk on
  tape ≥ 15 mm wide, on textured surfaces by their most chalked parts [4]; ISO 4628-8 measures the total widths of
  delamination and of corrosion separately, to 0.5 mm, at ≥ 6 points along a scribe [5]; ASTM D660 checking does
  not reach the substrate and D661 cracking does [39]; D4214 rates chalk 10 (none) to 0 [40]; JIS A 6909:2014
  degrees are CK0/1/3/5 (chalking), W0/1/3/5 (blistering), CD0–5 (cracking), GD0–5 (gloss) [15].

**Order with age.**
- **By finish** [C 15]. Thin finishes (リシン) have no top coat: they chalk and wear, then crack, lift and peel.
  Multilayer finishes (吹付タイル) start by top coat: solvent resins crack first, 2-pack resins chalk first,
  emulsions lose gloss first. Then: (1) the top coat chalks; (2) it cracks and peels; (3) it wears through while
  the main coat cracks and lifts; (4) the main coat peels. Thick finishes (スタッコ) without a top coat powder,
  expose their aggregate and lose it.
- **Old multi-coat paint** (NPS, on woodwork) [C 18]: crazing, fine jagged connected breaks in the top layer,
  comes when paint several coats thick turns hard and brittle; it goes on to cracking and alligatoring, and at last
  the surface flakes badly. Class I needs no paint removal; class II is a top-layer failure, class III a
  multi-layer one.
- **Timing.** Gloss loss is UV-driven, so it is judged against unsunned walls; chalking differs by orientation
  [C 15]. First repairs come at 10–15 years (rE B4); single-layer coats fall below the adhesion limit at about 17
  years (cracks note J38).

**Where each appears first.**
- **Soffits.** In a NILIM survey of 10 public housing blocks (built 1959–83), the photo captions put peeling and
  lifting under south balconies and in stair halls, blistering on a south face and cracks at balcony noses [P 16].
- **Protected areas** such as eaves and porches are not rinsed, salts build up, and the next coat peels between
  layers [C 18].
- **Tops**: on copings and tops where water stays, coats whiten and blister [P 19].
- **Water behind the coat** through unsealed cracks and joints peels, lifts and blisters it; efflorescence pushes
  coats off [C 15].
- **Recoats**: paint over a dirty or chalky surface peels; blisters grow from weak old layers left in place; tight
  new coats trap water as water blisters [C 15]. Rain streaks return to the same water paths after a repaint unless
  the top's drip is fixed, and microbial soiling recurs [C 15].

**Japanese repaint triggers** [P 17]. Otaru grades blistering, cracking, peeling and their mix by degree, area and
spread. A full repaint (大規模修繕) is called at degree ≥ 3 (protective coats, other finishes) or ≥ 2 (multilayer
and thick finishes, or where looks matter) over ≥ 20 % of a face, scattered; below 20 %, or local, a partial repair.
Wall cycles average 12–15 years (rE B31; cracks note J17–J18).

**What a flake exposes.** On repainted spray tile the commonest flake is the newest coat over an older chalky one
(type a) [C 15]. It shows the previous colour, often a hue step away (rE), then the white or cream, matte, textured
main coat; concrete (type b) shows last, mostly at soffits, tops and cracks [I]. Flakes are crisp-edged and curled,
in layers (r4-visual-reference §3).

## 3. Painted steel railings

**Where coatings break** (beyond rust note §1.2).
- **Edges and welds.** Surface tension and shrinkage pull wet paint away from sharp edges; most coating failures in
  ship ballast tanks sat on sharp edges, weld seams and spatter (snippet) [29]. Stripe coats on edges and welds
  should reach at least 2.5 cm in from the edge [P 29].
- **Pinpoints.** Pinpoint rust comes from thin film on profile peaks, edge defects from coating pulled away (snippet)
  [27]. ASTM D610 names spot, general, pinpoint and hybrid rust; ISO 4628-3 leaves edges out of its rating
  (snippets) [28].
- **Mill scale** [C 30] is bluish-black, usually < 1 mm, and cathodic to steel, so steel at a break in it corrodes
  faster; paint over it comes off with the scale once moist air gets underneath. Blasting (1種ケレン) is costly, so
  repaints use the simpler 2種–3種 tool grades (snippet) [19]; scale, old rust in pits and old film stay under the
  coats [I].
- **Repaint practice** [P 19]: blistered, cracked or lifted coats are removed with their surroundings; edges (where,
  the sheet says, rust starts easily) and rusted spots are spot-primed first; a current system is modified-epoxy
  primer plus two coats of 2-pack urethane (0.16–0.18 and 0.12–0.17 kg/m²). So repainted rails carry primer
  patches, later bumps, at edges, joints and feet [I].

**The visual sequence** [I, anchored as tagged]:
1. A chip or worn spot shows bare metal.
2. On wet steel, flash rust forms within minutes to hours; the moderate grade is a light tan-brown film that hides
   the metal, even or patchy (snippet) [26]. An outdoor chip is tan to orange within a day or two of rain.
3. Orange, then brown rust (lepidocrocite, then goethite; rust note §6).
4. Creep under the film (0.1–3.5 mm a year) lifts it as blisters; they crack and flake, showing rust, primer or scale.
5. Thick old rust has an ochre outer and a dark grey inner layer (rust note S38). Where the outer layer flakes, dark
   rust shows; fresh breaks show orange. This is the physical source of "dark intense" beside "bright new" rust.

**Bare metal** [I]. Bright metal lasts only where something keeps rubbing: the top rail's upper face in the hand zone
(0.8–1.2 m), where handrail paint wears through in a ragged strip 2–4 cm wide on the top and grip side
(r4-visual-reference §4); arris chips from bikes, poles and clamps; door latch edges. Elsewhere metal shows only in
the 0.3–3 cm rim between rust and paint, and reads dull: scale, primer, or freshly flash-rusted metal. Shares:
0.05–0.5 % of the steel at Typical, 0.3–2 % at Worn and Derelict, ≥ 60 % of it in the hand zone and at arris chips.

**Corrosivity and coating life.**
- **ISO 9223** first-year carbon-steel loss [C 22]: C2 10–200 g/m² (1.3–25 µm), C3 200–400 (25–50), C4 400–650
  (50–80), C5 650–1,500 (80–200). **ISO 12944-2**: C3 is urban or industrial air with moderate pollution, or a
  low-salinity coast; C4 is industrial or a moderate-salinity coast (snippet) [23].
- **Japan**: across 25 sites, steel lost a mean of about 180 g/m² (about 23 µm), on the C2/C3 border; zinc averaged
  6.96, a 26th of that [M 20] (the text was partly unreadable; these numbers were legible). At Choshi, sheltered
  specimens corroded more than rain-washed ones over 10 years; monthly loss peaks in summer and typhoons spike it
  [M 21], which supports the rust note's salty undersides.
- **Danchi** [I]: inland Tokyo is C3, within about 1–2 km of the sea C4. Rails are repainted every 5–7 years; ISO
  12944 lives run L ≤ 7 to M 7–15 years (rust note §5).

**Runs on vertical bars and horizontal rails.**
- **Horizontal rails**: water stands on the top face, which corrodes 2–3× faster than a vertical one (rust note
  S8); drops hang on the lower arris about 24 mm apart and stain the bar below; undersides are sheltered and salty
  [M 21].
- **Uprights** carry the top rail's water down as a film, and dissolved and colloidal iron leaves rust stain on the
  paint below each rusted joint (rust note S43; [I]). The film slows and pools on the bottom rail's top face and at
  the foot, where rust collars form (r4-visual-reference §3: worst where balusters meet a rail). Square bars also
  lose paint along their four arrises first, giving thin vertical rust lines [I from 29].
- **Two kinds of rust on an upright** [I]: a run is mostly stain on intact paint, and corrosion of the upright
  follows later along the same wet line. R3 caps "other upright faces" at ≤ 10 % of all rust up to 0.75, counting
  both. Unless the generator labels runs as stain, or R3's cap leaves run pixels out, the user's vertical streaks
  cannot pass.

## 4. Top edges, corners and recesses

**Parapet tops and top edges.**
- **PCI** [C 32]: a parapet of 200–300 mm normally stops roof water blowing over; its top should fall back toward
  the roof at 1:4 and be narrow, so the dirt on it does not streak the face; deposits form faster on upward faces
  and coarse textures. The kit's parapet top slopes inward 20 mm over 150 mm (rust note), so the outer face is fed
  by driving rain, drips off the outer arris, and cracks and joints in the top [I].
- **Japanese rain-streak sources** [P 33]: short coping overhangs, cracked mortar copings, unsealed coping joints;
  sills with short projections or no upturn; projections; joints (sealant leaching, double and inducer joints that
  trap dirt); slopes; ornament. Streaking showed on 2.7 % of 8,776 buildings in one 1 km district (snippet) [33].
- **Tops where water stays** whiten and blister the coat [P 19], and streaks return to the same paths after a
  repaint [C 15].

**Top corners.**
- **Rain** [C 32]: the top corners of the windward face are usually the wettest part of a building, then the top
  and side edges; corners can take 20–30× the rain impact of the face centre; and run-off seldom reaches the ground
  except at corners and projecting parts. The top quarter of a tower caught 41–47 % of the driving rain (rust note
  S31).
- **Stone versus paint.** On limestone the rain-washed parts are the white ones [M 34]; that needs an eroding
  surface. On paint the same wetness first washes the face pale (PCI's "white washing", r3 §4), the kept look.
  Unattended, it feeds biofilm: driving rain makes facades more prone to algae and can more than double the algae
  area in a year (snippets) [36]. So at Derelict the wet top corners should go dark and green-black, not pale [I,
  with organic note M3].
- **Damage.** Carbonation runs 1.2–1.35× the flat face at 2D corners and about 2× at 3D corners (snippets) [35], so
  top corners lose cover first and spalls should cluster there, an addition to K6 [I].

**External and internal corners.**
- **External**: secondary airflow concentrates run-off at the building's outside corners, at columns and at the
  inside corners of vertical projections; a corner with a long return needs a joint or groove to catch water blown
  around it, or the return is partly dirt-washed [C 32]. So: a washed strip with edge lines, and partial washing
  on the return [I].
- **Internal (re-entrant)**: they get the concentrated run-off and are shaded, still and slow to dry [C 32; I]. The
  organic note puts 0.1–0.4 m each side in the damp class W2; no study gave a measured width.

**Recesses and reveals.** Set-backs make rain washing very hard, and black crusts form there (snippet) [37].
Protected areas are not rinsed, so dust and salts stay and recoats peel [C 18]. A projection without a drip lets
water run back along the soffit and stain the wall; drips sit ≥ 38 mm from the edge; a drip stopped 50 mm short of
a window's sides gives a streak at each end [C 32]. Expected [I]: recess faces evenly dusty and darker with few
streaks, and their inner bottom corners (sill ends, floor lines) collecting dirt and growth.

**What Derelict should add** (the user: top edge and corners "a little bit more attention"; corners and recesses "a
tiny bit more growth and dirt") [I]:
- a dark, ragged band 0.05–0.6 m under the top: growth, drip streaks off the top arris, and peeled or blistered
  coat along 15–50 % of the top edge;
- top-corner zones about 1.5 × 1.5 m darker still, with green-black growth and more chips and spalls;
- inside corners and recess bottoms a little darker and greener than the open wall, fading out within about 0.6 m
  so that corners are not outlined.
