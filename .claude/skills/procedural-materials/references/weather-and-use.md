# Weather and use: where the environment and people act on a surface

Research for review 31, drawn from:
- the PCI weathering guide (DN-29);
- Blocken and Carmeliet on wind-driven rain;
- NPL and NIST edge-chipping tests;
- NPS preservation briefs;
- building codes for hardware heights;
- industrial-safety and floor-care guidance.

Numbers marked *(est.)* are estimates. Geometric damage from the same causes is in
`procedural-mesh-damage` (references/causes.md).

**The main rule: edges first.** Impact, water, wind, frost, sun and corrosion all concentrate at
arrises and corners. A chip or recess is an edge too, and inherits every rule below.

## Water

- **Tops collect, then shed at the edge.** Horizontal planes "collect the most dirt and are the
  most exposed to rain" (PCI). Run-off is slow, at most 0.9 m/min, and breaks into streams that
  recur in the same places at each rain, broaden at ledges and follow undersides.
- **Water leaves at the lowest point.** PCI: "Water will leave a drip at its lowest point." That
  point can be:
  - a sill end;
  - a sag in a slab edge;
  - a notch or chip in a top edge;
  - the lower rim of a recess.
- **Streak sources**, in order:
  1. the ends of horizontal elements and their junctions with verticals;
  2. local low points along every upward-facing edge, including the chips in it;
  3. soffits without a drip groove (surface tension carries water back underneath; projections
     of 300 mm or more need a drip 5–20 mm in from the front);
  4. the lower lips of recesses, openings and chips of 1 cm or more;
  5. joints and fixings.
- **A chip on a vertical face** keeps dirt on its sheltered upper wall, traps debris on its floor,
  and drips from its lower rim. Seed a short streak there.
- **Streak shape** *(est.)*:
  - length = k·√(catchment above), with k set so a 1 m sill gives 0.5–1.5 m;
  - the head as wide as the notch, widening 1.2–2× down the streak;
  - opacity falling as 1 − t^0.5;
  - fanned out at the next ledge, restarting from that ledge's ends.

  Drizzle gives pronounced random streaking; heavy rain washes.
- **Soffits:** without a drip, a dirt band underneath and a streak below its inner line; with a
  drip, the soffit stays clean.
- **What photographs of Japanese apartment blocks show** (review 31, 23 facades):
  - **Every streak starts at a feature.** In 17 of 17 photos: sill ends and lower window
    corners (6), horizontal tops with no drip (6), attachments such as vent hoods, rail posts
    and duct covers (5).
  - **Sill end:** 3–8 cm wide at the origin, fanning out at 10–20° to 15–25 cm, darkest in the
    first 10–30 cm, ending at 0.5–1.5 m. The two ends of a sill make a V, and the wall under the
    sill's middle stays cleaner.
  - **Top with no drip:** a dark band 5–15 cm under the lip, then a fringe of streaks 0.5–3 cm
    wide, spaced irregularly 2–30 cm apart. Most stop at 20–40 % of the face height; a few reach
    the bottom.
  - **Inner faces of balcony and corridor parapets** carry a hair-fine fringe every 1–3 cm over
    their full height. Outer faces below a drip lip are clean.
  - **Vertical attachments and corners** get a dark line down each side edge, not a centre
    streak. A removed duct cover leaves a clean ghost strip with dirty margins.
  - **Rail posts on a slab edge**, about every 1.2 m, each give a spall at the post pocket and a
    rust streak down the whole slab face, with efflorescence drips between.
  - **At 30–60 m** the streaks sort into columns that line up with the openings. The walls
    between them and the building corners read paler, and the parapet carries a dark band. About
    1–2 window columns in 5 are much worse than the rest, through several floors.
  - **Pale washed streaks** showed in only 2 photos, as a late-life feature.
- **Streak rules from the photographs:**
  - spawn streaks only from features;
  - give each source a seeded strength, so 20–30 % of the sources dominate;
  - draw lengths from a power law: a median of 0.3–0.5 m with a 5 % tail of 2–3 m;
  - use a fringe only on a horizontal top with no drip;
  - vary strength per column of openings, not per bay.
- **Streak variety** (the user's round-B picks, review 33). Every round so far failed here,
  even with sourced streaks. Vary each of these independently, per source:
  - **Spacing.** Real sources are irregular, so streaks under a long edge never sit at even
    intervals. Jitter the low points along the edge, merge neighbours closer than 5–10 cm, and
    leave gaps of 0.5–2 m with nothing at all.
  - **Length.** Most streaks are short or partial. Some stop a quarter or half of the way to the
    next ledge, and a few reach the ground. Use the power law above, and also end streaks at the
    next sill, band or joint, where the water is caught or spreads out.
  - **Width and shape.** Not all long and narrow. Where water collects (the end of a sag, an
    inside corner, under a wide sill) the streak is wide: 15–60 cm, a soft fan or a curtain.
    Narrow 1–3 cm runs come from a notch or a fixing.
  - **Intensity and colour.** A few dark, most faint. Hue shifts by cause: soot brown-grey,
    green-black where damp stays, rust orange under steel, pale where washed.
  - **Build-up.** A large stain is never one flat shape. Build it from 3–6 overlapping streaks
    at different opacities (10–60 %), with lighter gaps between runs and a darker core where
    the runs overlap. A single uniform dark sheet reads as paint, as in the v4 bay.
  - **No mid-wall starts.** A streak that begins in the middle of a plain 90° wall reads as a
    mistake. Start it at a feature, a joint or a chip, or let it fade in from a ledge above.
  - **Not over-clustered.** 80/20 does not mean every streak in one patch. The v4 gable
    clustered its streaks so tightly that the rest of the wall looked unweathered. Keep a
    background of faint short streaks across all exposed tops.
- **Height on the facade.** The top floors and the parapet get the most driven rain, so more
  water runs through every source up there: the top-floor and parapet streaks are the longest
  and strongest, and the band under the parapet is the darkest. Open faces on the top floors
  are also washed paler, so the contrast between streak and wall is highest there. Lower floors
  get less rain and more sheltered, unwashed grime. The user: "rain would gather mostly at the
  top floors and a little less at the bottom". Scale streak strength and length by about
  1.5–2× from the ground floor to the top floor *(est.)*.
- **Inside corners collect.** Water, debris and dust gather where two walls meet, where a
  balcony wall meets the slab, and at the inner corners of parapets. Give them more of every
  layer: a wider streak down the corner, dirt in the corner line, damp and growth at the
  bottom. This is the one place on a facade where water gathers rather than sheds.
- **Frost** needs more than 75–80 % saturation, so it scales and rounds the outer top edges of
  copings, sills, parapets and steps, where water sits.
- **Base:** rising damp ends at a salt tide mark 0.5–1.5 m up (typically about 1 m), with salt
  spalling where evaporation concentrates.
- **Biology:** on north and east faces, below sills, at drip landings and at leaking gutters.
  Orange *Xanthoria* lichen marks bird perches.

## Wind

- **Driven rain reaches the edges first.** Blocken and Carmeliet: "the top corners are most
  wetted, followed by the top and side edges." Windward vertical edges are washed pale, with flow
  concentrated down the corner.
- **Shelter keeps the dirt.** Black crusts survive only where rain cannot wash: under cornices and
  cantilevers, and in the tops of recesses. Sheltered steel can corrode 3–5× faster, because salt
  is not rinsed off.
- **Blown sand** stays mostly below 0.5 m, with almost none above 2 m. It abrades and undercuts
  bases.
- **Rules:**
  - choose one prevailing wind direction per site;
  - windward vertical edges get a washed band 0.1–0.5 m wide (value +3–8 %);
  - the lee side gets dust and soot;
  - crust goes under projections and in recess tops;
  - on dusty or coastal sites, abrade windward vertical edges, peaking at 0.1–0.3 m and gone by
    1 m.

## Sun and temperature

- **UV fades horizontal faces fastest** and edges about 1.2× faster than the face *(est.)*. Wood
  greys only 0.05–0.5 mm deep.
- **Fading and colour drift in paint.** Sun-facing walls lose chroma and gain value (chalking),
  so the south and west faces of one building differ slightly from the north. A repainted
  patch shows as a hue or sheen shift. Even a clean, recently painted wall is never one flat
  colour: roller laps, panel-to-panel batch shifts and dust give it a soft variation of
  ±2–4 % value at 0.3–3 m. The user called the v5 walls "too flat" for lacking this.
- **Paint fails by element.** From NPS Preservation Brief 10:
  - eaves peel;
  - sills and balusters crack and alligator;
  - wet paint pulls back from sharp edges, so edges and welds take 50–90 % of the corrosion
    attacks on painted steel.

  Peeling grows inward from edges and water sources.
- **Cracks start at re-entrant corners of openings:** about one per 2–4 openings, running 30–60°
  off the corner bisector, 0.3–1.5 m long and tapering *(est.)*. No crack starts mid-face.
  Crazing (hexagons of 40 mm or less) appears on smooth tops only.

## Painted steel: water decides where it fails

From photographs of railings, doors and shutters on Japanese apartment blocks and warehouses
(review 31):
- **Railings** (9 of 10) rust worst on the top surfaces of horizontal rails, where balusters
  meet a rail, and at post bases, which carry a black and rust halo of 5–10 cm on the concrete.
  A run falls below each junction. Vertical balusters stay mostly sound.
- **Doors** fail in the bottom 10–30 cm and along the lock or hinge edge. Paint comes off in
  crisp curled flakes, in layers: topcoat, then an older light paint, then rust. Large faces
  only chalk and fade evenly.
- **Shutters** rust in horizontal streaks along one to three slat interlocks, not all of them;
  the bottom rail and the bottom 20–30 cm of the guides are worst.
- **Rust runs** are 1–4 cm wide at the source and 0.3–1.5 m long, burnt orange turning
  brown-grey. On corrugated sheet they follow the valleys and end in teardrops.
- **Rules:**
  - failure mask = upward-facing horizontal members + within 8–15 cm of junctions + the bottom
    0.3 m + door bottoms and lock edges; elsewhere only chalk;
  - each spot drives a rust bloom, a blister halo 1.5–2× its size, and a run;
  - peeling is crisp-edged flakes over a lighter older layer, never a soft gradient.

## Gravity and accumulation

- **Dust** = N.y² × occlusion × (1 − washed). It peaks in inside corners and at the backs of
  level ledges, and is cleared within 2–5 cm of a rain-exposed outer edge.
- **Debris** collects in inside corners. Droppings land 0.1–0.5 m below about 1 in 5 perches:
  rail tops, ledges, sign tops.
- **Moss and lichen** grow only on damp, shaded tops and at drip landings.

## People: touch points come from building codes

Building codes fix the heights of hardware, rails, buttons and slots. Protection products
(kick plates, rack guards, crash rails) mark where the knocks land.

| Touch point | Height, size | What wears |
|---|---|---|
| Lever handle | 865–1220 mm (US), 900–1100 mm (UK) | polish along the top and outer ⅔ of the lever, strongest at the tip; a grime ring at the rose |
| Push plate | 300 × 75 mm, centred 864–1219 mm | palm prints 100 × 150 mm clustered above and beside the plate (people miss it); a grease fan toward the latch edge |
| Door latch stile | 0.9–1.5 m | a fingermark band 50–150 mm wide along the latch edge on the pull side; the hinge side stays clean |
| Door foot | kick plates usually 8 in (about 200 mm) | scuffs 0–250 mm on the push side; cart strikes up to 1.2 m |
| Light switch | 700–1200 mm | a grey halo 20–60 mm around the plate, heavier on the approach side |
| Lift buttons | 900–1200 mm | the legend worn in a 6–12 mm disc; in busy lifts the door-close button wears most |
| Handrail | grip top 865–965 mm; runs 305 mm past the top riser | the top 120–180° of the section polished, strongest in the first 0.5–1 m at each end and at landings |
| Keypad | 0.9–1.4 m | 3–5 keys polished, their legends gone |
| Letter slot | 700–1700 mm | the flap's lower lip polished, scratches fanning 20–40 mm below |
| Keyhole | — | a scratch arc of 15–40 mm radius, heavier below the hole |
| Stair tread | — | nosing wear only 20–25 mm deep on the tread, plus about 10 mm down its face; a lane 40–60 % of the width toward the rail; dished 1–5 mm in concrete, 10–40 mm in old stone |
| Floor near entrances | — | about 42 % of the finish worn off within six feet of a door. Lanes go dull and scuffed; edges build up finish and grime, with a dark line at the skirting |
| Walls | — | kick scuffs 0–250 mm; chair backs 0.7–0.9 m; trolley bands around 0.2–1.0 m |

**What photographs show** (review 31): touch wear is a band or strip, not a disc.
- **Doors:** on 4 of 5, grime is a vertical band 10–20 cm wide on the latch edge at about
  0.8–1.7 m, darkest on the door's edge face, with more above the handle than below.
- **Switches:** the grime is offset toward the side people approach from, at 1.5–3× the plate
  size.
- **Handrails:** the paint is worn through in a ragged strip 2–4 cm wide on the top and grip
  side, worst mid-flight and on the starting newel cap, with the underside intact.
- **Lifts:** one or two buttons, such as the ground floor or door-open, wear far more than the
  rest. Brass is polished in the centre, with patina left at the rim.
- **Stairs:** wear sits beside the handrail or on the inside of a turn, dished in the front half
  of the tread. The sides stay sharp, with moss where nobody steps.

Give touch targets power-law usage weights, so one or two take most of the wear. Shapes: an
edge band (doors), an offset fan (switches), a top strip (rails), a centre polish (buttons), a
front-half dish (treads).

**How materials respond** to one hand-contact field *(est. except where noted)*:

| Material | At touch points |
|---|---|
| Unlacquered brass | rubbed zones stay gold-bright (roughness 0.2–0.3); recesses go dark brown. Patina shows after about 10 days of use (sourced) |
| Lacquered brass | lacquer worn through: a bright patch with a tarnished ring at the lacquer edge |
| Anodised aluminium | paler, roughness 0.15–0.25 lower |
| Painted steel | paint, then primer, then bare steel. Constantly handled steel stays dark and polished; rarely touched exposures rust |
| Varnished wood | roughness rises sharply where touched most (sourced); gloss dulls and matte shines, both toward 0.35–0.5 |
| Wall paint, stainless | skin oil holds dirt: albedo 10–25 % darker; grease prints on stainless |

**Urban marks** *(est.)*:
- **Graffiti:** writers choose spots that maximise exposure (sourced, Ghent study). Tags mostly at
  0.8–2.2 m on shutters, doors, utility boxes and sign backs. Buffed rectangles in a near-match
  colour sit over old tags, with new tags on the buff.
- **Stickers:** at 1.2–1.8 m on poles and boxes, in torn generations that leave adhesive outlines
  holding dirt.
- **Gum:** black discs 10–25 mm, densest within 2–5 m of stops and doors.
- **Bikes against a wall:** tyre rubber at 0–0.7 m, pedal gouges at 0.2–0.4 m, bar scuffs at
  0.9–1.1 m.
- **Pots:** rust or salt rings the size of the base, 2–3 of them overlapping.
- **AC streaks** start at the condensate drain outlets of indoor units, not under every outdoor
  unit.

| Cause | Where | Texture |
|---|---|---|
| Rack upright hits | mostly below 300 mm (sourced) | paint scraped to metal, rust where damp |
| Dock door tracks | the bottom 0.6–0.9 m is struck most often (sourced) | bent, scraped |

| Floor markings | lines 2–6 in wide | survive in straight runs, fail at crossings, turns and dock aprons |
| Oil, hydraulics | under parked machines | pools 0.2–0.6 m, darkest at the centre with a soft halo; −30–50 % albedo on concrete; roughness −0.2–0.4 when fresh; tracked 3–10 m by tyres |
| Battery charging | — | etched lighter concrete, exposed aggregate, white or yellow crust |
| Heat tint on stainless | around flanges and exhausts | pale yellow 290 °C, straw 340, brown 390, purple 450, blue 540, dark blue 600 °C (sourced); a soot plume above flues, widening upward |
| Galvanising | crevices and laps where moisture sits | white rust; elsewhere weathered zinc is seldom attacked (sourced) |
| Column bases | the bottom 0–150 mm, worse on the wet side | rust at the grout line |
| Dirt gradient | from the loading door | 70–80 % of dirt enters at doors (sourced); density halves every 5–15 m, raised again in lanes |

**Post-industrial decay** follows water. Masonry outlasts roofs and floors (sourced); the
timeline is an estimate:
- **0–2 years:** blocked gutters, leaks under roof penetrations, ground-level vandalism.
- **2–10 years:** ponding, rust runs, blown paint, soft timber, moss, droppings under openings.
- **10–30 years:** roof sheets lost, floors failing below leaks, trees in gutters.

## Distribution: 80/20 for use

- **Touch is concentrated.** In one hospital study only five surfaces were high-touch. Rank the
  touch points: main entrance > core doors > handrail ends > unit doors > service doors.
- **Traffic funnels.** Trails bundle into a few shared paths (Helbing), and wear sits at
  entrances, pinch points, turns and stair starts.
- **Infer touch points from function.** Every operable part is a touch point at its code height.
  Every opening has a push side, a pull side and a foot zone. Every door, stair, lift or dock is a
  path endpoint. Vertical edges within 0.6 m of a turn take strikes at the mover's bumper height.
- **Store it in the generator:**
  - **Per piece:** an `interaction` point cloud authored with the piece, carrying `P`, `N`,
    `kind` (grip, push, press, slot, foot, strike), `radius`, `side` and `use_weight`.
  - **Per building:** a path graph (entrances → cores → units) accumulated into a floor
    `traffic` attribute, blurred 0.3–1 m, raised to a power of 2–3 to bundle.
  - **Fields:**
    - `touch = use_weight × nearby traffic × rank`. The top 15–25 % are hot; the rest stay at
      10–30 %.
    - `strike = traffic × convexity × (1 − dist / d)`, with d = 0.3 m for feet and carts and
      0.6–1.0 m for forklifts.

## Shader layers that read these fields *(est.)*

| Layer | Mask | Albedo | Roughness | Metal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Grip polish on metal | `touch`, tight (r 30–80 mm), stretched along the grip | brass +15–30 % toward gold | −0.2–0.35 | as base |
| Grease on paint, stone, wall | `touch`, a halo 1.5–3× the polish radius | −10–25 %, toward warm grey-brown | −0.05–0.15 | 0 |
| Paint, then primer, then metal | `touch` or `strike`, thresholds 0.5 / 0.75 / 0.9 | red-oxide primer (about 0.20, 0.07, 0.04 linear) or grey; bare steel about 0.55 | primer 0.7; steel 0.3 if touched, rust if struck and damp | 1 on bare steel |
| Floor lane | `traffic` | finish worn, value +3–8 %; black scuffs at turns | +0.1 on gloss floors | 0 |
| Floor edge | 1 − `traffic`, near walls | yellowed, darker within 0.3 m of the skirting | −0.1 | 0 |
| Streak | streak sources above | toward the run-off dirt colour, or paler where washed | +0.05 | 0 |

**Checks:** touch layers sit at code heights; the hot points hold 70–85 % of each layer; no contact
wear where nothing is operable or nobody walks; every streak has a source above it.
